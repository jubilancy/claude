## A Saint, Her Book, and a Convent in Woodcut: St Bridget of Sweden at the British Museum

Tucked away in the British Museum's Prints and Drawings department — currently not on display — is a small, quietly extraordinary object: a hand-coloured woodcut of St Bridget of Sweden, printed in Augsburg sometime between 1480 and 1500. Registration number 1934,0609.2-4. It's not a famous piece. But it's a window into how a religious order chose to represent its own founder to the world, and it carries the fingerprints of shifting German politics stamped right into the paper.

### What the print shows

The composition is built from a single carved block, cut into three sheets and mounted together — a common approach for large devotional prints of the period, since it let printers work at a bigger scale than a single block of wood would easily allow.

At the centre, Bridget sits on a bench holding an open book in each hand. A crown rests at her feet. An angel stands at her right shoulder. Above her, the scene splits into a small celestial court: God the Father appears on a cloud to the upper left, and the Virgin and Child on a cloud to the upper right — Bridget receiving a kind of double blessing, framed on both sides.

Below her float the coats of arms of Sweden, Bavaria, and the Palatinate — a heraldic footnote that turns out to be the most historically loaded detail in the whole piece (more on that below).

On the left sheet, eight Brigittine nuns kneel in prayer, folded hands turned toward the centre. At the front of that line kneels a specific, named figure: Bridget's own daughter, St Catherine of Sweden. On the right sheet, a mirroring group of eight praying monks kneels toward her as well.

Read as a whole, the print is doing something quite deliberate: it's showing St Bridget *giving her rule to her order* — codifying, in a single image, the moment her authority as founder passes down into the community of monks and nuns who followed her.

### Who was St Bridget?

Bridget of Sweden founded the Bridgettine Order (the Order of the Most Holy Saviour) in the fourteenth century, and her double monasteries — housing both monks and nuns under a shared rule — were unusual enough to leave a visible trace in prints exactly like this one. Her daughter, Catherine of Sweden, is given a place of honour in the composition, kneeling closest to her mother, which suggests the print was likely made for or by a Bridgettine house wanting to assert its founding lineage visually.

### The politics hiding in the coat of arms

Here's the detail that turns this from a devotional image into a small historical puzzle. The Museum's curatorial notes identify this impression as the **second state** of the woodcut — meaning the block was altered after its first printing. Specifically, the shields of Bavaria and the Palatinate were swapped in to replace the arms of Wittelsbach and Oettingen.

Scholarship (Schreiber) places that change after 1488, the year the Countess of Oettingen stepped down as abbess of the Kirchheim convent. In other words: the print's heraldry was quietly updated to match a change in who held religious authority in the region — a woodcut functioning almost like a modern institution updating its letterhead, except carved permanently into wood.

### Provenance: from a Bavarian collection to Bloomsbury

The print's paper trail is unusually well documented. It came from the Oettingen-Wallerstein collection at Maihingen, sold at auction by Karl & Faber in Munich on 11 May 1934. The British Museum's own print curator at the time, Campbell Dodgson, attended that sale — and the acquisition notes suggest he was instrumental in securing it, persuading collector Henry Van den Bergh to fund the purchase, with the Art Fund (then NACF) supporting the acquisition as well. It was registered into the collection less than a month later, on 9 June 1934.

Since then it's travelled: exhibited at Karlsruhe in 2002 as part of *Iconoclash*, then in the 2005 *Origins of European Printmaking* exhibition that moved between Washington's National Gallery of Art and the Germanisches Nationalmuseum in Nuremberg, and most recently to Brisbane's Queensland Museum in 2015–16 for *Medieval Europe: Power and Legacy*.

### Why a small, undisplayed print still matters

Nothing about this object is monumental. It's roughly the size of a large book page, hand-coloured rather than painted, made anonymously by a German printmaker whose name is lost. But that's exactly what makes it useful as a historical document: it shows how a religious order visually built and revised its own institutional memory — literally re-carving its allegiances into the block — in the earliest decades of European printmaking, when a single woodblock, split across three sheets, was still a technically ambitious way to tell a founding story.
